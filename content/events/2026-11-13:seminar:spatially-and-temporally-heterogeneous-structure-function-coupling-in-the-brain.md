Structure-function coupling is not uniform across the brain: it varies regionally in ways that reflect cortical hierarchy, cytoarchitecture, and a multiplex of underlying neurobiological mechanisms. The sources and consequences of this heterogeneity remain incompletely understood. Our recent work addresses this gap across several converging lines of inquiry. Challenging the classical static view, coupling strength fluctuates moment-to-moment, with intermediate hierarchical regions exhibiting the greatest temporal variability. These temporal signatures are further differentiated across imaging modalities: comparing MEG- and fMRI-derived coupling reveals systematic organization along hierarchical gradients and frequency bands, with different bands better captured by different communication models, suggesting that distinct neurophysiological processes operate across the spatiotemporal scales linking structure to function. Incorporating biological detail further improves explanatory power. Neurotransmitter receptor profiles enhance prediction of functional connectivity particularly in unimodal cortex, while neuropeptide receptor-ligand pair distributions shed light on distinct signalling and communication mechanisms. These molecular signaling pathways may also extend beyond neural transmission to modulate the vasculature, influencing the dynamic coupling between cerebral structure and blood perfusion. Yet capturing these biological relationships requires methodological care: systematic benchmarking across hundreds of functional connectivity measures shows that structure-function correspondence shifts with analytical choices, potentially affecting sensitivity to individual differences. Together, these findings position coupling heterogeneity as a biologically grounded and methodologically contingent window into the mechanisms shaping neural communication.

Dr Zhen-Qi Liu is a postdoctoral researcher in the Systems Neuroscience Laboratory at the University of Cambridge. He is a Royal Society Newton International Fellow and a Junior Research Fellow at Wolfson College.
